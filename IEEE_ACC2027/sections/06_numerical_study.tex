\section{Numerical study}\label{sec:sim}
Figure~\ref{fig:profile} illustrates the predicted swing profiles. The team has four $1.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ quadrotors carrying a $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ point payload on $1\,\mathrm m$ cables, with $T_{\min}=1\,\mathrm N$, $f_{\max}=44\,\mathrm N$, $\bar T_i=3.2\,\mathrm N$, $\rho_i=34\,\mathrm N$, $a_{\max}=13\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, $\theta_q=20^\circ$, $\bar z=0.239$, $\bar w=0.171$, $\bar\omega=1\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and $\nu=\nu_w=0.5\,\mathrm{s^{-2}}$. The thrust-reserve bound holds: $\sqrt{(\bar T_i+m_ia_{\max}+m_il_i\bar\omega^2)^2+\rho_i^2}=41.73\,\mathrm N<f_{\max}$. The code and trial videos are available online.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/Hadi-Hajieghrary/Authority_Barriers}} Throughout, $\gamma(s)=s$, the initial states are drawn with seed 11, the taut-cable model is integrated between updates with the eighth-order Dormand--Prince method at relative and absolute tolerances $10^{-9}$ and $10^{-11}$, and the programs are solved by Clarabel at its default tolerances through Drake 1.51.1 in Python 3.10.

\emph{Exact model.} Theorem~\ref{thm:sandwich}(i) was tested in its own setting: the authority filter \eqref{eq:filter} on the taut-cable model under a zero-order hold, with $T^{\mathrm h}_i=2.7\,\mathrm N$, $\nu'=0.4\,\mathrm{s^{-2}}$, $c_\downarrow=5$ and $c_\uparrow=2\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, $\epsilon=0.1\,\mathrm s$, $c_a=1.25$, and the band $0\le\chi\le45\,\mathrm m$. The 100 initial states have random swing states, speeds of $1$--$4\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $H(x_0)\in[0,0.05\,\Dres(x_0)]$, between $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $0.38\,\mathrm m$; the nominal input is adversarial: the full thrust of every quadrotor toward the wall, tilted $40^\circ$ upward; a trial lasts $6\,\mathrm s$. Table~\ref{tab:exact} gives the result. With a period of $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ the barrier stayed above $-0.05\,\mathrm{mm}$, one trial reached the wall, by $2\,\mu\mathrm m$, and the altitude stayed in its band in every trial; with $0.5\,\mathrm{ms}$ no trial reached the wall and the barrier stayed above $0.22\,\mathrm{mm}$; the payload climbed by up to $29.9\,\mathrm m$.

The contact is a hold effect: rerun from the same state with the same nominal input, the trial stops $0.26\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the wall with a hold of $0.5\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $0.39\,\mathrm{mm}$ with $0.25\,\mathrm{ms}$, whereas tightening the integrator tolerances to $10^{-12}$ or the solver tolerances to $10^{-10}$ at $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ changes the penetration by less than $1\,\mathrm{nm}$; the three other states that reach the wall with $5\,\mathrm{ms}$ keep clearances of $0.27$--$1.7\,\mathrm{mm}$ at $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $0.31$--$2.3\,\mathrm{mm}$ at $0.25\,\mathrm{ms}$. Between updates (ten states per hold) the minima of $h$, $H$, and the altitude barriers equal those at the updates to the printed digits, the swing states leave their sets by at most $2\cdot10^{-7}$, and every command respects the tension floor and the thrust limit. The certificate is continuous-time; its sampled implementation carries a margin that shrinks with the hold.

Figure~\ref{fig:exact} compares the authority filter with HOCBF filters with linear gains $(k_1,k_2)\in\{(0.5,10),(1,20),(2,40)\}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ from one state with $v=3\,\mathrm{m/s}$, every cable at $z_i=-0.8\bar z$, and $h=1.02\,\Dres=7.76\,\mathrm m$. Each HOCBF program is feasible at the start and infeasible after $0.15$, $0.52$, and $0.78\,\mathrm s$, with every cable leaning toward the wall; its relaxation with a penalized slack reaches the wall at $7.3$--$9.2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The authority filter has every cable in the braking half-space after $0.81\,\mathrm s$ and stops the payload $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the wall, with $H$ between $0.15$ and $0.01\,\mathrm m$. The bound on the swing accelerations in \eqref{eq:filter-box} is what keeps the hold benign: without it the filter follows the nominal input with swing accelerations of about $20\,\mathrm{s^{-2}}$, forty times $\nu$, and the stopping distance changes within one period by more than its gradient predicts; a margin of the kind derived for sampled-data CBFs~\cite{breeden2022sampled} is not available for $\Dres$. With the hover floor, the stopping distance from $3\,\mathrm{m/s}$ with every cable at $-\bar z$ is $9.18\,\mathrm m$, against $6.56\,\mathrm m$ with the floor $T_{\min}$ and the time-optimal swing: carrying the weight costs $2.6\,\mathrm m$.

The remaining trials use Full-order model simulations with rigid quadrotors, spring--damper cables that can go slack, thrust vectors applied directly or through a $1\,\mathrm{kHz}$ attitude loop, and a filter at $200\,\mathrm{Hz}$ with the wall barrier alone (the floor $T_{\min}$ in $\phi_i$, the time-optimal swing, and neither \eqref{eq:filter-alt} nor the bound on the swing accelerations). All 690 HOCBF trials lost QP feasibility before a cable entered the braking half-space: the three gain pairs of Fig.~\ref{fig:exact}, each from 100 states with every cable leaning toward the wall at $2$--$4\,\mathrm{m/s}$ under both actuation models, and from 30 states with three quadrotors; this does not establish initial feasibility for arbitrary gains. This model lies outside the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:sandwich}: a cable that goes slack within a $5\,\mathrm{ms}$ hold can cost the barrier up to $0.85\,\mathrm m$, so the sampled filter needs an empirical margin; in a sweep of 100 states under the adversarial nominal input, no trial started with $H(x_0)\ge0.78\,\mathrm m$ reached the wall. The margin holds only for the tested distribution.

Using the Clarabel solver in Python, evaluating $\Dres$ and its gradients takes less than $1,\mathrm{ms}$ on one core of an Intel i7-1165G7. The complete filter takes a median of $3.5,\mathrm{ms}$, with a maximum of $4.1,\mathrm{ms}$ for $N=4$, meeting the $5,\mathrm{ms}$ control period used in the full-order trials. In comparison, a backup filter based on numerical integration of $\plan$ \cite{chen2021backup} reproduces $\Dres$ with a relative error of $10^{-6}$ and the control commands within $0.4,\%$ of $f_{\max}$. However, using central-difference gradients, this approach takes $129 \,\mathrm{s}$ per evaluation, making it about four orders of magnitude slower. These results demonstrate computational feasibility within the tested setup, but a real-time guarantee would additionally require bounds on solver failures, model mismatch, and inter-sample excursions.
