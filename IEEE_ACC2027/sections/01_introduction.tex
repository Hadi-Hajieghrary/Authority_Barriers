\section{Introduction}
%A quadrotor can pull a cable-suspended payload but cannot push it. If all quadrotors are between a moving payload and a wall, changing tensions cannot brake the payload; a quadrotor must first swing to its far side (Fig.~\ref{fig:scene}). A high-order control barrier function (HOCBF) hides this delay by testing only the currently achievable acceleration. We quantify the delay and construct a safety certificate from the maximum wallward excursion of a feasible braking maneuver. This creates a recursive-feasibility problem: an input may satisfy the safety filter now even though the cable geometry cannot change quickly enough to preserve sufficient braking authority later. We therefore seek (i) an explicit controlled-invariant set that accounts for this reconfiguration delay, (ii) an explicit outer bound on the viability kernel that identifies states from which no admissible controller can remain safe, and (iii) a safety filter that is guaranteed feasible throughout the certified set.

%Input-constrained HOCBFs~\cite{xiao2022high,breeden2021high}, feasibility constraints~\cite{xiao2022sufficient}, and backup, constructive, and predictive CBFs~\cite{chen2021backup,vanwijk2025,squires2018constructive,breeden2022predictive} address feasibility through different safe sets; backup CBFs keep racing drones inside a geofence~\cite{singletary2022onboard}, and the braking-distance barrier of adaptive cruise control~\cite{ames2014control} is the special case of a constant authority. Cable-robot wrench calculations describe forces available at a fixed geometry, as intervals or zonotopes~\cite{gouttefarde2011interval,bouchard2010ability,erskine2019wrench}; aerial transport models describe how that geometry moves~\cite{sreenath2013dynamics,lee2018geometric}, and CBFs have been used to plan cable transport~\cite{pallar2025optimal}. Concurrently,~\cite{shi2026escape} certifies a quadrotor that must reorient its thrust before it can brake, with a first-order body-rate model; here the authority follows from the cable geometry, the swing is second-order, and the same calculation gives an outer bound. Closed-form backup control is also studied in~\cite{vanwijk2025}; our closed form concerns the future wall excursion for this particular authority model.


%We let each quadrotor reserve part of its thrust for cable tension and part for swinging the cable; the braking authority this guarantees is the support function of the zonotope of tension forces and depends only on the cable directions (Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}). When every cable swings to its limit, the payload covers at most the stopping distance $\Dres$ toward the wall, which is computed exactly (Sec.~\ref{sec:barrier}). The authority barrier is $H:=h-\Dres$: states with $H\ge0$ and admissible swing states form a controlled-invariant set in which the braking maneuver satisfies every constraint of the filter in Sec.~\ref{sec:filter}; two further barriers bound the payload altitude during the maneuver. With upper bounds on the authority and on the swing acceleration, the same calculation gives an optimistic stopping distance $\Drel$, below which no state is viable (Theorem~\ref{thm:sandwich}). Both bounds hold for every system whose braking authority is bounded by nondecreasing functions of coordinates that the inputs drive only through their second derivative (Sec.~\ref{sec:class}); the cable system is one instance.

%The contributions are fourfold. First, we characterize when the failure motivating the paper must occur: an initially feasible HOCBF filter can lose feasibility before any cable reaches the braking half-space if the geometry cannot reconfigure quickly enough (Theorem~\ref{thm:negative}). Second, we derive an explicit stopping distance for a feasible thrust-reserved swing-and-brake maneuver in the input-constrained taut-cable model and use it to construct the authority and altitude barriers; together they define a controlled-invariant certified set on which the proposed barrier filter remains feasible (Theorem~\ref{thm:sandwich}(i), Proposition~\ref{prop:instance}). Third, using optimistic bounds on braking authority and swing acceleration, we derive an explicit outer bound on the viability kernel (Theorem~\ref{thm:sandwich}(ii)). Thus, the inner and outer constructions bracket the unknown viability kernel using quantities that are computable online. Fourth, simulations test the certificate on its own model, compare it with HOCBF filters, quantify the conservatism of the certified set, and expose the limitations of a direct sampled implementation. In simulation, the HOCBF filter lost feasibility in all 690 trials; on the exact model with a $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ hold, the authority filter reached the wall in one of 100 trials started at the boundary of its certified set, by $2\,\mu\mathrm m$; on the full-order model with a $5\,\mathrm{ms}$ hold and the wall barrier alone, it reached the wall in 59 of 100. The sampled results concern the implementation and give no safety guarantee.

Cooperative aerial transport uses multiple quadrotors to carry a payload through cables.  A cable can pull the payload but cannot push it.  The braking forces available to the team therefore depend on the cable directions.  If every cable pulls toward a wall, the team must reposition before it can slow the payload's approach (Fig.~\ref{fig:scene}).  The payload can continue accelerating toward the wall during this motion.  Safe braking must therefore account for the time needed to change the cable geometry.

Safety filters restrict a nominal controller's commands to enforce prescribed state constraints.  A wall-distance high-order control barrier function (HOCBF) filter can admit a control command now and lose feasibility later.  Feasibility here means that at least one command satisfies both the filter constraint and the physical input limits.  Preserving this admissibility over time is a recursive-feasibility problem.  We investigate how much wall clearance is needed when braking requires a prior change in configuration.

\subsection{Related Work}

High-order and exponential control barrier functions address safety constraints whose inputs enter through higher derivatives \cite{xiao2019control,xiao2022high,nguyen2016exponential}.  Input-constrained formulations construct safety conditions that account for limits on the available control \cite{breeden2021high}.  Additional feasibility constraints can preserve admissible control choices over time \cite{xiao2022sufficient}.  These results establish that feasibility must be considered alongside satisfaction of the physical safety constraint.

Backup CBFs construct controlled-invariant sets using the future motion under a known backup controller \cite{chen2021backup,gurriet2020scalable}.  Constructive and predictive barriers also use future trajectories to formulate safety conditions \cite{squires2018constructive,breeden2022predictive}.  Backup methods have been applied to high-speed drone geofencing \cite{singletary2022onboard}.  Van Wijk et al. derive a closed-form controller that interpolates between nominal and backup commands \cite{vanwijk2025}.  This result concerns the computation of the control command.

For cable-driven robots, wrench-feasibility analyses characterize the forces available at a given geometry \cite{gouttefarde2011interval,bouchard2010ability,erskine2019wrench}.  Cooperative aerial-transport models describe the dynamics that govern changes in cable geometry \cite{sreenath2013dynamics,lee2018geometric}.  CBF-based trajectory planning has also been developed for obstacle avoidance during cooperative payload manipulation \cite{pallar2025optimal}.  These approaches provide relevant tools for analyzing force availability and planning safe motion.

Shi et al. address the time needed for a quadrotor to reorient its thrust before braking \cite{shi2026escape}.  Their escape-aware barrier accounts for reorientation limits imposed by bounded body rates.  The cable-transport setting considered here couples braking forces to cable coordinates with bounded second-order dynamics.  Our focus is an explicit calculation of the future wallward excursion caused by this reconfiguration delay.  The calculation supports complementary sufficient and necessary conditions for avoiding wall contact.

\subsection{Contributions}

The paper makes four contributions.  First, we establish a sufficient condition for an initially feasible wall-distance HOCBF filter to lose feasibility before braking becomes available (Theorem~\ref{thm:negative}).

Second, we derive an explicit stopping-distance certificate from a thrust-reserved swing-and-brake maneuver.  It defines a controlled-invariant set with guaranteed filter feasibility under the stated continuous-time taut-cable assumptions (Theorem~\ref{thm:sandwich}(i), Proposition~\ref{prop:instance}).

Third, we derive an optimistic stopping distance that bounds the viability kernel from outside.  It identifies states from which no admissible controller can avoid wall contact (Theorem~\ref{thm:sandwich}(ii)).

Fourth, simulations evaluate the filter against HOCBF and numerical backup-CBF baselines.  They illustrate conservatism and expose safety violations under sampled control and slack-cable dynamics.
