\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Cable geometry makes braking authority a controlled state that changes at a bounded rate. An initially feasible HOCBF filter can lose feasibility when the geometry cannot change quickly enough, and an explicit swing-and-brake maneuver gives continuous-time inner and outer viability bounds for every system with second-order authority. The inner bound is conservative where the cables must first swing: safe trajectories were found from as little as $0.25\,\Dres$ there, against $0.97$--$0.98\,\Dres$ with the cables already behind the payload; the reserved swing acceleration accounts for part of this gap.

On the exact model, the certificate holds up to the hold: with a period of $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ and bounded swing accelerations, one of 100 trials started at the boundary of the certified set reached the wall by $2\,\mu\mathrm m$, none did with $0.5\,\mathrm{ms}$, and four did with $5\,\mathrm{ms}$. The full-order trials, run with the wall barrier alone, a $5\,\mathrm{ms}$ hold, and cables that can go slack, do not satisfy the hypotheses of the theorem: the barrier lost up to $0.85\,\mathrm m$ within one hold, 56 of 100 trials had a slack cable, and 59 reached the wall. A certified sampled-data bound, full-order trials with the altitude barriers, a less conservative swing reserve, and a distributed implementation are the next requirements for a deployment claim.
